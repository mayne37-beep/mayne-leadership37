Course Roadmap

One discipline. Three levels of thinking.

Individual assumptions enter group conversations. Group decisions alter systems. System feedback then influences future assumptions. The course moves through all three levels before bringing them back together.

By the end, you can
1
Examine how you form and revise knowledge.
2
Use practices that reduce groupthink and widen participation.
3
Move a team from divergence to convergence without forcing false consensus.
4
Distinguish a visible symptom from an underlying system problem.
5
Explain not only what you recommend, but how the thinking arrived there.

Level 1 · Self

NOTICE YOUR THINKING

Pressure rewards speed. Complexity punishes shortcuts. The pause is not indecision; it is quality control.

Thinking About Thinking

Awareness without adjustment is observation.

Metacognition adds regulation: notice what is happening in your reasoning, name what is shaping it, and navigate toward a better next move.

The purpose is not to eliminate intuition or speed. It is to notice when a fast answer is becoming an untested answer.
Calibration Challenge

Confidence is a feeling. Evidence is a reason.

No searching · No discussion
Who was the 19th President of the United States?
0%50%
Answer: Rutherford B. Hayes.

The point is not trivia. Compare your certainty with the quality of the evidence you actually had.

Memory can feel vivid and still be wrong. Social confidence can masquerade as factual confidence. Good judgment includes calibration—matching certainty to the evidence available.
Evidence · Authority · Closure

Three forces shape what we accept as knowledge.

Think about a current non-sensitive decision. Rate how strongly each force is shaping your judgment right now.

EVIDENCE

What information supports the claim?

AUTHORITY

Whom or what do I trust in this domain?

CLOSURE

How urgently do I feel I need a settled answer?

A source can be credible in one domain and weak in another. Urgency can also make the first plausible explanation feel more certain than it deserves.
90-Second Closure Check

Separate urgency from evidence.

This is a private reflection, not a diagnostic score. Need for closure can support timely action; the risk appears when the urge to settle suppresses search, dissent, or revision.

Watch for “seize and freeze”: accept an answer quickly under uncertainty, then defend it after it becomes familiar.
Changing Your Mind Is Evidence of Learning

Model revision as strength, not inconsistency.

Think of a belief, judgment, or recommendation you revised.

Leadership signal: “Here is what changed my mind.” When leaders explain revision, they teach that evidence can outrank ego.
Level 2 · Group

INVITE DISSENT

A team can contain diverse expertise and still produce narrow thinking if the process makes silence safer than contribution.

Groupthink + Silence

The absence of disagreement is not proof of agreement.

Select the two pressures most likely to silence useful thinking in your environment.

Groupthink is not healthy teamwork or legitimate consensus. The problem is premature, protected consensus—when concerns stay private, assumptions go untested, alternatives disappear, and risk looks smaller than it is.
Leaders Can Design for Candor

Let the process carry some of the interpersonal risk.

Open all five leader moves, then choose one you could add to a recurring meeting.

Psychological safety develops through behavior over time. Structured facilitation can also reduce the immediate cost of speaking up before that culture is fully developed.
Disciplined Decision Process

Make the thinking visible before making it final.

1 · FRAME

What problem are we solving?

2 · DIVERGE

What else could be true?

3 · MAKE SENSE

What patterns and assumptions matter?

4 · CONVERGE

What best fits our criteria?

5 · COMMIT

Who does what, by when?

The Groan Zone

Productive discomfort is not automatically a process failure.

DIVERGENCEDifferent frames and possibilities expand.
GROAN ZONEConfusion, friction, repetition, integration.
CONVERGENCEShared understanding supports narrowing.
Do not rescue the team from productive discomfort by imposing false consensus. Keep participation equitable, the task clear, and the purpose visible.
Premortem

Imagine the decision failed six months from now.

Scenario: your organization launches a new employee-development initiative. Six months later, participation is low and supervisors say it disrupted operations. Generate three plausible reasons before defending the plan.

Premortems legitimize doubt before people become invested in defending the plan. They separate criticism of a plan from criticism of a person.
Gradients of Agreement

Reveal more than yes or no.

Imagine the proposed initiative from the premortem is still being considered. Pre-commit to one position before reading anyone else's view.

Use the rating to open the conversation, not replace it. Hear each person once, begin with outliers, and allow revision after the reasoning is heard.
Dot Voting

Prioritize. Do not pretend the vote decided truth.

Choose two options you would want the team to analyze more deeply. The vote focuses attention; it does not validate the original framing.

Protect independence by pre-committing before seeing others' votes. After voting, test the leading options against criteria and use gradients of agreement to surface reservations.
Integrated Case

BIKE TRAIL CONFLICT

A visible incident sits inside a crowded, multi-stakeholder system.

Case adapted from a 1993 Washington Post report

Do not solve the tack incident alone.

A heavily used Northern Virginia trail is experiencing repeated safety problems. Hundreds of bicycle tires have reportedly been punctured by deliberately scattered tacks. At the same time, cyclists, runners, walkers, and inline skaters are competing for limited space on a narrow shared path. Users describe collisions, arguments, different expectations about right-of-way, and disagreement about what the trail is for. Authorities respond to visible incidents, but the broader conflict continues.

VISIBLE EVENT

Repeated tacks and large numbers of flat tires create an urgent safety and enforcement problem.

RECURRING PATTERN

Crowding and conflict among multiple users existed beyond the tack incidents.

SYSTEM QUESTION

How do design, norms, enforcement, capacity, behavior, and stakeholder expectations interact?

Step 1 · Think–Write

Frame before the group influences you.

Step 2 · Circle of Voices · Online Adaptation

Preserve different frames before combining them.

Open all four perspectives. Do not decide which is “right” yet. Listen for what each frame makes visible that another could miss.

Step 3 · Cluster → Criteria → Vote → Gradient

Narrow without erasing the thinking.

Select at least three criteria for judging a response.

Generate four plausible response options.

Pre-commit to the two options you would analyze further.

Step 4 · Debrief the Process

Process evidence matters.

A decision is stronger when you can explain how alternatives were tested—not only which answer won.
Level 3 · System

SEE THE SYSTEM

Every fix enters a living system. A useful solution changes the pattern without quietly weakening the system.

Systems Lens

Watch for the chain, not just the event.

The original course uses this short systems-thinking cautionary tale. As you watch, capture the original problem, the interventions, the delayed effects, and what the original frame omitted.

A systems lens asks different questions.

Shifting the Burden

Relief now can quietly weaken the real solution.

Use the bike-trail case—or another non-sensitive workplace issue—to test whether a quick response is masking or weakening a deeper response.

Shifting the Burden is a hypothesis to test, not proof of causation. Systems thinking without feedback becomes speculation.
Systems Leadership

The strongest response may combine relief and reform.

STABILIZE NOW

Reduce immediate harm and restore safe operations.

Guardrail: do not let emergency relief become the permanent operating model.

BUILD CAPACITY

Change the conditions that recreate the symptom.

Guardrail: define evidence, ownership, resources, and time horizons.
