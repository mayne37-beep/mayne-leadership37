Better thinking begins before better decisions.

Every day, we interpret information, make assumptions, solve problems, and make decisions. The question isn't whether you think. The question is how well you examine your thinking before acting on it.

Chapter 1

FOUNDATIONS

Before improving your thinking, understand what critical thinking actually is.

What is critical thinking?

Disciplined thinking used to improve the quality of our judgments and decisions.

It requires us to deliberately examine:

  • What do I know?
  • How do I know it?
  • What am I assuming?
  • What else could explain this?
  • Does the evidence support my conclusion?
Critical thinking isn't about thinking more. It's about thinking better.

Chapter 2

BARRIERS

Good people can make poor decisions for perfectly understandable reasons.

Your Brain Is Efficient

Thinking shortcuts are useful—until they aren't.

Human thinking relies on shortcuts. That's usually useful. We recognize patterns, draw on experience, and make thousands of judgments without consciously analyzing each one.

But efficiency has a cost. Under pressure, uncertainty, emotion, or familiarity, a useful shortcut can become a thinking trap.

Critical thinkers don't eliminate these tendencies. They learn to notice them.
Thinking Trap Gallery

Six traps worth recognizing.

Open each one. The goal is not memorizing labels—it is recognizing what the trap looks like in your own reasoning.

01
Confirmation Bias

We favor information that supports what we already believe and discount evidence that challenges it.

02
Anchoring

Early information becomes a reference point and can influence later judgment more than it deserves.

03
Availability

Recent, vivid, or memorable information feels more important or more common than it may actually be.

04
Groupthink

The desire for agreement or cohesion suppresses questions, alternatives, and constructive disagreement.

05
Premature Closure

We stop searching once we find an explanation that seems good enough—even when important questions remain.

06
Overconfidence

Our confidence in a conclusion exceeds the quality or completeness of the evidence supporting it.

Beyond Bias

Not every barrier has a psychological label.

The environment around us can make disciplined thinking harder. Open each factor.

TIME PRESSURE

Urgency can push us toward the first workable explanation rather than the best-supported one.

EMOTION

Strong emotion can narrow attention and change which evidence feels important.

AUTHORITY

Rank or expertise can discourage people from testing assumptions or voicing contradictory evidence.

GROUP PRESSURE

Social costs can make agreement easier than constructive challenge.

INFORMATION OVERLOAD

More information is not always better; volume can hide relevance and weaken prioritization.

EXPERIENCE

Experience accelerates pattern recognition, but familiar patterns can cause us to overlook what is different this time.

Chapter 3

PROCESS

A disciplined path from uncertainty to defensible judgment.

The Critical Thinking Journey

Structure your thinking without turning it into a rigid formula.

Critical thinking is iterative. New information may send you backward. A question may change how you define the problem. Analysis may reveal an unknown that requires more evidence.

DEFINE — Clarify the actual problem before attempting to solve it.
01

Problem or symptom?

Solving the wrong problem efficiently is still solving the wrong problem.

A useful problem statement describes the gap without embedding an assumed cause or preferred solution.

INWARD

A Caution About Root Causes

Not every complex problem has one root cause.

Searching for the root cause can encourage premature certainty when outcomes are actually produced by several interacting conditions.

A stronger question is often: “What combination of conditions could be producing this outcome?”
