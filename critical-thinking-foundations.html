CRITICAL THINKING · FOUNDATIONS

Better thinking begins before better decisions.

Every day, we interpret information, make assumptions, solve problems, and make decisions. The question isn't whether you think. The question is how well you examine your thinking before acting on it.

Chapter 1

FOUNDATIONS

Before improving your thinking, understand what critical thinking actually is.

What is critical thinking?

Disciplined thinking used to improve the quality of our judgments and decisions.

It requires us to deliberately examine:

  • What do I know?
  • How do I know it?
  • What am I assuming?
  • What else could explain this?
  • Does the evidence support my conclusion?
Critical thinking isn't about thinking more. It's about thinking better.

Chapter 2

BARRIERS

Good people can make poor decisions for perfectly understandable reasons.

Your Brain Is Efficient

Thinking shortcuts are useful—until they aren't.

Human thinking relies on shortcuts. That's usually useful. We recognize patterns, draw on experience, and make thousands of judgments without consciously analyzing each one.

But efficiency has a cost. Under pressure, uncertainty, emotion, or familiarity, a useful shortcut can become a thinking trap.

Critical thinkers don't eliminate these tendencies. They learn to notice them.
Thinking Trap Gallery

Six traps worth recognizing.

Open each one. The goal is not memorizing labels—it is recognizing what the trap looks like in your own reasoning.

01
Confirmation Bias

We favor information that supports what we already believe and discount evidence that challenges it.

02
Anchoring

Early information becomes a reference point and can influence later judgment more than it deserves.

03
Availability

Recent, vivid, or memorable information feels more important or more common than it may actually be.

04
Groupthink

The desire for agreement or cohesion suppresses questions, alternatives, and constructive disagreement.

05
Premature Closure

We stop searching once we find an explanation that seems good enough—even when important questions remain.

06
Overconfidence

Our confidence in a conclusion exceeds the quality or completeness of the evidence supporting it.

Beyond Bias

Not every barrier has a psychological label.

The environment around us can make disciplined thinking harder. Open each factor.

TIME PRESSURE

EMOTION

Strong emotion can narrow attention and change which evidence feels important.

AUTHORITY

Rank or expertise can discourage people from testing assumptions or voicing contradictory evidence.

GROUP PRESSURE

Social costs can make agreement easier than constructive challenge.

INFORMATION OVERLOAD

More information is not always better; volume can hide relevance and weaken prioritization.

EXPERIENCE

Experience accelerates pattern recognition, but familiar patterns can cause us to overlook what is different this time.

Chapter 3

PROCESS

A disciplined path from uncertainty to defensible judgment.

The Critical Thinking Journey

Structure your thinking without turning it into a rigid formula.

Critical thinking is iterative. New information may send you backward. A question may change how you define the problem. Analysis may reveal an unknown that requires more evidence.

DEFINE — Clarify the actual problem before attempting to solve it.
01

DEFINE

Problem or symptom?

Solving the wrong problem efficiently is still solving the wrong problem.

A useful problem statement describes the gap without embedding an assumed cause or preferred solution.

“Employees need more training.” What is it?

Correct: “Employees need more training” already assumes both the cause and the remedy. A more neutral statement would describe the performance gap itself.

A Caution About Root Causes

Not every complex problem has one root cause.

Searching for the root cause can encourage premature certainty when outcomes are actually produced by several interacting conditions.

A stronger question is often: “What combination of conditions could be producing this outcome?”

From Analysis to Judgment

Diverge before you converge.

Strong judgment requires alternatives, criteria, evidence, and an honest look at tradeoffs.

05

EXPLORE

Create alternatives before choosing.

Our first reasonable idea often gains an unfair advantage simply because it arrived first.

Delay commitment long enough to create alternatives.

Practice: generate three possible explanations.

A team's processing time has increased for three months. Before selecting a cause, generate three plausible explanations.

Good. We didn't ask you to find the best idea first. We prevented the first reasonable idea from automatically becoming the answer.
06

EVALUATE

Criteria before choice.

If you decide what you prefer before deciding what matters, your criteria can become justification rather than evaluation.

Define what a good decision must accomplish before comparing alternatives.

Choose exactly three criteria first.

07

DECIDE

Make judgment visible.

A defensible decision does not require everyone to agree. It requires others to understand how the judgment was reached.

OPTION+CRITERIA+EVIDENCE+TRADEOFFS→DEFENSIBLE JUDGMENT

Confidence should match evidence.

How confident are you in your current judgment?

Confidence: 7/10

New information: A second data source contradicts one of the assumptions supporting your preferred option.

Adjust your confidence if the evidence changes what the conclusion deserves.

08

REFLECT

Close the loop.

Reflection is not simply asking whether the decision worked. It examines the quality of the reasoning that produced it.

Outcome

What happened after the decision?

Reasoning

Which assumptions, evidence, or alternatives mattered most?

Learning

What would you repeat or change next time?

Tool Discipline

Good tool use begins before you open the template.

Use a tool when…

You need structure for a specific reasoning task: finding relationships, testing assumptions, comparing alternatives, organizing evidence, or seeing stakeholders.

Do not use a tool just because…

It is familiar, impressive, required by habit, or available. A polished framework can create false confidence if it is answering the wrong question.

The simplest adequate method is often the strongest. Sometimes the right “tool” is a better question or one missing piece of evidence.

Chapter 5 · Application

YOU KNOW THE LANGUAGE.

One organizational challenge. Eight stages. Your reasoning determines what happens next.

Case File · Project Relay

A performance problem—or something else?

A service unit that coordinates time-sensitive requests has missed its 24-hour processing target for ten consecutive weeks.

Leadership's initial proposal: mandatory refresher training for all employees.

Your job is not to defend or reject that proposal. Your job is to determine what the evidence supports.

Initial Operating Picture

What you know at the start.

Performance: On-time processing fell from 91% to 73% over ten weeks.
Workload: Incoming volume increased 8% during the same period.
Change: A new intake-and-routing procedure began eleven weeks ago.
Training: 94% of employees completed required procedure training before launch.
Employee reports: Several employees describe conflicting guidance about priority cases.
Unknown: No one has compared delays by shift, case type, routing path, or supervisor.
Resist the urge to solve the case now. Your information will expand as your questions improve.

01

DEFINE

State the performance gap without embedding a cause or solution.

Which is the strongest problem statement?

The neutral performance gap is strongest. The other choices embed either a remedy or an unproven cause.
02

UNDERSTAND

Separate evidence from interpretation and identify what remains unknown.

Which statement is an inference?

Correct. The training explanation may be plausible, but the available evidence does not establish it.
03

QUESTION

Ask for information that can distinguish between competing explanations.

Which question has the greatest diagnostic value?

Strong questions help discriminate among explanations. This question can show where the performance gap is actually being produced.
New evidence received:

Most delays occur in priority cases routed through two approval paths. Those cases average 31 hours; other cases average 19 hours. The two affected paths require an additional supervisor review introduced with the new procedure. Error rates are essentially unchanged from before implementation.

04

Which explanation is now best supported?

The evidence now points most strongly to a process bottleneck. That does not prove there are no other contributors, but it should change where attention goes next.

Create three distinct response options.

Aim for genuinely different approaches—not three versions of the same idea.
