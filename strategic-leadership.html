Strategic Leadership Premise

SHAPE THE CONDITIONS

The work shifts from solving tasks to creating clarity, alignment, trust, and systems that preserve intent.

Big Idea

Your method of influence changes as the system grows.

Personal control → Alignment

You cannot personally direct every decision. You create intent, boundaries, priorities, and relationships so others can move together.

Tasks → Conditions

The strategic leader increasingly shapes systems, resources, structures, decision rights, incentives, and communication rhythms.

Individual execution → Leader network

Success depends on second-level leaders and partners who understand enough intent to act well when you are absent.

What works with a small team can fail at scale: personally solving every issue, relying on informal communication, or assuming intent is understood because it was said once.

Levels of leadership

Open all four. The same character matters at every level; the application changes.

Strategic leaders do not outgrow the foundation. Character, competence, self-awareness, and trust become more consequential as scope and indirect influence grow.
What Changes as Leaders Move Up?

The job moves from doing the work to creating the conditions for the work.

Personal influence→Indirect influence
Immediate outcomes→Long-term conditions
Control→Alignment
Answers→Better questions
Work→Systems
Personal expertise→Integrated expertise

Which transition is hardest for strong performers?

A strength at one level can become a liability at another. Decisiveness can become premature closure; expertise can become over-control; responsiveness can keep the leader permanently on the dance floor.
What Leaders Bring · What Leaders Do

Strategic scope increases the cost of weak foundations.

Character

Values, ethics, discipline, accountability, consistency, and the willingness to place mission and stewardship above ego.

Presence

Credibility, resilience, calm under scrutiny, confidence without arrogance, and consistency across audiences.

Intellect

Judgment, innovation, mental agility, systems understanding, and the ability to integrate expert advice.

Competencies still come down to leading, developing, and achieving—but the scale, time horizon, and indirect nature of influence change dramatically.
Federal Leadership Connection

Strategic leadership is visible in the current Executive Core Qualifications.

For federal leaders, OPM's current ECQs connect rule of law, efficiency, merit and competence, leading people, and achieving results. Strategic thinking is explicitly included under Achieving Results.

The leadership challenge is not simply to move faster. It is to achieve legitimate, effective, accountable outcomes while managing people, resources, technology, risk, and long-term organizational interests.
Strategic Leader Meta-Competencies

Six behaviors help leaders operate at enterprise scale.

Open all six. The framework is adapted from strategic-leadership research and translated into practical leader behavior.

Meta-Competency 1 · Mental Agility

Change altitude without losing judgment.

Strategic leaders move between details, systems, and future implications. They can hold competing truths long enough to understand them before locking onto an answer.

Mental agility is not knowing everything. It is integrating expertise without becoming trapped in one frame.
Meta-Competency 2 · Formulating Vision

Vision turns complexity into direction.

A strategic vision is not a slogan. It describes a desired future condition clearly enough to guide decisions when the leader is absent.

Weak: “We need to improve communication.”
Stronger: Describe the future condition, why it matters, and what decisions it should guide.
The test of vision is not whether it sounds inspiring. The test is whether people can use it to make aligned decisions when you are not in the room.
Meta-Competency 3 · Making and Shaping Decisions

Sometimes you decide. Sometimes you shape the decision environment.

Strategic leaders clarify decision rights, frame choices, surface tradeoffs, and make the type of request explicit.

Use explicit language: is this a recommendation, a decision, a request for guidance, or a request for concurrence? Unclear decision rights create delay and shadow decision-making.
Meta-Competency 4 · Building Successful Teams

Strategic teams often include experts you do not command.

Select the conditions your cross-functional team most needs to strengthen, then design the operating environment around them.

Cross-functional teams exist because no single viewpoint sees the whole system. Trust, clarity, cadence, and psychological safety are operating requirements—not extras.
Meta-Competency 5 · Communicating Effectively

Same strategy. Different audience needs.

Choose a hypothetical organizational change. Keep the core truth consistent while changing emphasis, language, and information for five audiences.

Strategic communication is disciplined translation. Adapt the emphasis without changing the integrity of the message. Build feedback loops so the message can travel back as well as out.
Meta-Competency 6 · Ethical Ambition

Accomplish the mission without separating results from character.

Strategic leadership has to hold effectiveness, legality, policy, stewardship, people, risk, accountability, and trust together.

Influence Without Control

Responsibility often extends beyond formal authority.

LEAD DOWN

Create clarity, resources, trust, development, and accountability.

LEAD UP

Frame issues, surface risk, provide options, and improve senior leaders' understanding.

LEAD ACROSS

Build coalitions, shared understanding, agreements, and reciprocal trust with peers.

LEAD OUT

Coordinate with partners, customers, stakeholders, communities, and external systems.

Unity of effort without unity of command is strategic leadership terrain. Authority helps, but alignment also depends on relationships, credibility, timing, evidence, shared purpose, and trust.
Public Federal Case · Admiral Thad Allen

Leading a complex system under scrutiny.

During the Deepwater Horizon response, Admiral Thad Allen served as National Incident Commander. The environment included federal agencies, state and local governments, private industry, scientific experts, elected officials, affected communities, media, and the public. No single leader commanded the entire system.

Strategic leadership is not only command. It is integration: aligning stakeholders with different authorities, information, incentives, risks, and definitions of success.
Application

Where do you need unity of effort without full control?

Think of cross-functional initiatives, technology implementation, workforce development, training, policy, legal coordination, headquarters-field relationships, or partner work.

Strategic Leadership Failure Modes

Strategic failure can look like leadership activity.

Select the two failure modes that create the most risk in your environment.

Meetings, taskers, slide decks, and messages are not alignment by themselves. Ask whether decisions are clearer, roles are understood, risk is visible, feedback is flowing, and people know what matters most.
Leader Tool · Strategic Alignment Dashboard

Alignment must be visible before it can be managed.

Use a real or hypothetical initiative. Could every stakeholder state the same intent, priorities, risks, and next learning point?

If different stakeholders carry different assumptions about intent, priorities, risk, or decision timing, alignment is weaker than it appears.
Capstone · Project Convergence

Lead a three-year enterprise modernization effort.

A fictional organization is implementing a major modernization effort affecting people, policy, technology, training, field operations, headquarters, and external partners. Existing mission work must continue. Resources are limited, trust varies, timelines compete, and success depends on adoption—not merely deployment.

The job is not to create a technical implementation plan. The job is to create strategic alignment: clear intent, disciplined priorities, visible stakeholders and risk, and leader behavior that supports adoption.
Senior-Leader Brief Template

Brief the thinking—not just the recommendation.

Your brief should make the desired condition, the things that must hold, the strategic risks, and the decision needed immediately visible.

If the brief becomes too tactical, ask: What strategic condition are we trying to create? If it becomes too abstract, ask: What decision or leader action is required now?
