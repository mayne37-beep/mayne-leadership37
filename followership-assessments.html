About this model and its two dimensions

Kelley describes follower behavior using two dimensions: independent critical thinking and active engagement. The intersection is commonly discussed in terms of five styles. The questionnaire below uses original learning items, not Kelley’s published wording.

Independent Critical Thinking

Evaluating information, forming your own professional judgment, and considering alternatives rather than accepting ideas automatically.

Active Engagement

Contributing energy, initiative, responsibility, and meaningful participation in the work of the team.

About this model and its two dimensions

Chaleff emphasizes the shared purpose and the follower’s responsibility to both support and constructively challenge leaders. This instructional assessment looks at those two dimensions rather than assigning a permanent personality label.

Courage to Support

Acting responsibly, following through, contributing to the mission, and helping leaders and colleagues accomplish the right work.

Courage to Challenge

Raising concerns, questioning risky decisions constructively, and taking appropriate action when values or the shared purpose are threatened.

Explore the four followership styles and descriptions
High support • High challenge

Partner

Supports the leader and mission while maintaining the courage to question direction. Growth focus: combine candid judgment with constructive action.

High support • Lower challenge

Implementer

Provides dependable execution, but may be reluctant to raise uncomfortable concerns. Growth focus: practice respectful, evidence-based voice.

Lower support • High challenge

Individualist

Challenges weak ideas and speaks independently, but may not visibly help carry the solution. Growth focus: pair candor with practical support.

Lower support • Lower challenge

Resource

Contributes within defined limits without much additional initiative or challenge. Growth focus: increase purposeful engagement and use your judgment more actively.

Assessment notes, limitations, privacy, and sources

How to interpret these instructional assessments

Status of these questionnaires: Both 20-item forms are newly written educational self-reflection tools inspired by the distinct Kelley and Chaleff models. They are not the authors’ published questionnaires, and no evidence has yet been collected establishing these items’ reliability, factorial validity, fairness, or predictive validity. Do not use the scores for hiring, promotion, discipline, employee evaluation, or other high-stakes decisions.

Response instructions: Use the most recent three months. Select a frequency only when you had an opportunity to demonstrate the behavior. “Not observed” is an unscored response rather than Never. Up to two “Not observed” items are allowed on each 10-item dimension before the result is classified as having insufficient evidence.

Transparent scoring: Each model measures two different dimensions with 10 items per dimension: five direct-scored and five reverse-scored. Responses range from 0 to 6. A direct item scores its rating and a reverse item scores 6 minus its rating. To handle a small number of “Not observed” responses, the average of observed item scores is multiplied by 10 to yield an estimated 0–60 score. The result reports how many items were observed. Neither scores nor cutoffs are norm-referenced.

Quality and uncertainty: Repeated identical answers, substantial disagreement between related item pairs, limited opportunity to observe behavior, and scores close to the quadrant cutoffs trigger interpretive cautions. These are instructional checks, not tested fraud-detection or psychometric confidence measures. A moderate position does not prove someone is a Pragmatist, and the chart is not a personality diagnosis.

Pilot validation recommended: Before describing these as validated, have subject-matter experts map each item to the intended construct, conduct cognitive interviews with diverse employees, pilot with an adequately sized voluntary sample, check reliability by dimension (for example, omega), inspect item-total relationships and two-factor structure, examine response bias and group fairness, and revise scoring cutoffs using evidence rather than a fixed midpoint. Re-test revised forms with a separate sample.

Privacy: The self-assessments run entirely inside this HTML file, without network calls, tracking, accounts, or browser storage. Responses vanish when the page is refreshed or closed. Printing or saving a PDF is controlled by the student.

References: R. E. Kelley, The Power of Followership (1992); I. Chaleff, The Courageous Follower; International Test Commission / Association of Test Publishers (2025), Guidelines for Technology-Based Assessment. These are conceptual and practice references, not validation of this new instrument.
